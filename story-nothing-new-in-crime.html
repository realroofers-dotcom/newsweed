Nothing new in crime: an old Polish trick, and the stock market

When communism ended in Poland, insiders took state companies cheaply. Newsweed's owner says the same pattern now runs through small public companies, and he asked a Polish AI what to call it.

Newsweed · November 14, 2026

When Poland left its state-run economy around 1989, members of the old ruling class, the nomenklatura, took control of state companies and moved them into private hands. Poles call it "uwłaszczenie nomenklatury." In English it is usually called nomenklatura privatization.

Mark Nejmeh, who owns Newsweed, runs a theory site, triggeredshort.com. It argues that the same pattern can be run on a public company, with its stockholders in place of the state: good news lifts the stock, warrants and short selling sell into the rise, the price falls, and the value ends up in fewer hands. He calls it the Triggered Short.

He asked a Polish AI

He put the question to Bielik, a Polish AI assistant. He described a company whose stockholders' money is spent down on purpose, whose products are shelved just before completion, and which is then taken over cheaply while new money finishes the products. He asked: "What would you call that, Mr. Bielik?"

"This could be referred to as a 'hostile takeover' or 'raider takeover' ... a form of corporate raiding, where the goal is to acquire the company at a low price." — Bielik

Then he asked what it is when the company's own management does it, using good news, warrants and short selling.

"This is a form of market manipulation and is considered unethical and potentially illegal." — Bielik

Asked whether it had been seen outside Poland, the assistant pointed to Russia in the 1990s, where a loan scheme let insiders take control of state companies and created the oligarchs. It said Mr. Nejmeh's observation that the same "reverse trick" shows up in the United States and Europe "is not entirely unfounded."

What to make of it

These are a chatbot's answers, not findings, and Bielik itself ends each conversation with "Bielik sometimes makes mistakes." The theory site says plainly what the public record can show, such as the warrant terms, the dates shares were registered, the share counts and the trading volume, and what it cannot show: who sold, who was short, and why. The full conversation is published, unedited except for spelling in the questions, at triggeredshort.com.

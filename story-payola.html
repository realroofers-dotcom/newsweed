When your lover tells you “I love you, I just slept with your best friend”: how a stock analyst rolls

Payola for Your Pills: 56 drug and biotech companies raised $6.53 billion in four months. The banks that sold the shares, whose analysts also publish the research, were paid $309.4 million in fees.

Newsweed · November 20, 2026

A new special report from 8K10Q.com, “Payola for Your Pills: How Stock Analysts Get Paid,” reads every final offering prospectus filed by drug and biotech companies from July 15 to November 19, 2026. In those four months, 56 companies raised $6.53 billion from investors in 62 stock sales. The banks that sold the shares were paid $309.4 million in cash fees, as the prospectuses themselves state.

At November 18 closing prices, 44 of the 57 stocks with a price record traded below where they closed the day before their raise. In two of the raises, China SXT Pharmaceuticals sold warrants that can be turned into up to 135 million shares for no cash at all; the company itself says it expects no cash from them.

Who pays the analyst

The banks that publish research on drug companies are the same banks collecting those fees. FINRA’s own rule bars paying an analyst for a specific deal, but allows his pay to depend on the firm’s investment banking revenue, as long as the research report discloses it (Rule 2241(c)(4)(B)). The report puts each bank’s fee in plain view, raise by raise.

“It’s like your lover telling you, ‘I love you. I slept with your best friend,’” said Mark Nejmeh, founder of 8K10Q.com. “The analyst says buy, and the same filing shows his firm got paid to sell you the shares. Every investor should know this game, for every stock on the market.”

One company from the record

When Theriva Biologics was named Pipex, it paid Crystal Research Associates $35,000 while Crystal’s co-founder, Jeffrey Kraws, was a Pipex director and vice president (Form 10-KSB/A, August 29, 2008). Analysts from A.G.P., Maxim Group and H.C. Wainwright, all of them banks that sell biotech shares, have covered Theriva. Theriva is the defendant in Nejmeh v. Theriva Biologics, Inc., 3:26-cv-00705 (D. Nev.); in its November 12, 2026 quarterly report, Theriva says the lawsuit made it “aware of the fact that the definitive proxy statement for the 2026 Annual Meeting contained errors.”

The bigger number

“Based on my calculations, the wealth gap comes partly from stock losses caused by facts hidden in the filings,” Mr. Nejmeh said. “Companies that used warrants alone lost their investors $16.54 trillion. Everyone better wake up, because as AI repositions your job, investing has to become part of your income.”

The $16.54 trillion is WarrantWire.com’s count from every annual report filed with the SEC from 2001 to 2026: what 7,696 companies that used warrants were worth at the start, plus the money they raised, minus what is left today.

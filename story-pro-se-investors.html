No lawyer, still heard: the investors who take companies to federal court themselves

Every year a few dozen shareholders file securities cases in federal court without a lawyer. The odds are long. One of them is Newsweed's owner.

Newsweed · November 10, 2026

You do not need a law firm to be heard in a federal courtroom. Every year, people who own a few shares of a company file their own cases, write their own papers and argue against companies represented by large law firms. The court calls them "pro se," meaning they speak for themselves.

How many there are

The Federal Judicial Center, the research arm of the federal courts, keeps a public record of every civil case filed since 1988. Newsweed counted the securities cases and stockholder suits in it that list a self-represented plaintiff. The record runs through June 30, 2026.

Those numbers are larger than the real count of people like the ones in this story. The court marks a case as self-represented if any plaintiff in it has no lawyer, so some are law-firm class actions in which one investor filed a paper on their own. Many cases filed as "securities" are really about credit cards, car loans or mortgages. Newsweed's first sort of the case names, not yet checked docket by docket, finds roughly 20 to 30 cases from January 2024 to June 2026 in which one stockholder sued a public company over its own stock. The court's records use no single label for those cases, so this count is only an estimate. Some involve the biggest companies on the market.

The odds

From January 2024 through June 2026, 237 cases of both kinds carried the self-represented flag. By June 30, 2026, 107 were still open. Of the 130 that had closed, none reached a trial and 3 settled. The rest ended in other ways: dismissed on a motion, withdrawn, sent to another court or closed for lack of activity.

The courts do give people without lawyers some room. The Supreme Court has said that a document filed by a self-represented person is "to be liberally construed." The deadlines and the rules are the same for everyone.

One of them

Mark Nejmeh owns Newsweed. He is an inventor and an early internet builder. On September 16, 2026, he sued Theriva Biologics, Inc., a drug company based in Rockville, Maryland and listed on the NYSE American under the symbol TOVX, in federal court in Nevada. He has no lawyer. The case is Nejmeh v. Theriva Biologics, Inc., No. 3:26-cv-00705, in the U.S. District Court for the District of Nevada.

On November 9 he filed his First Amended Verified Complaint, which adds four Theriva directors as defendants. It alleges a pattern of reverse stock splits, discounted warrants and share sales that he says favored a small group of funds over ordinary stockholders. It also alleges that a stockholder vote approving new warrants passed on a quorum that existed only because of broker shares voted on other proposals, and under a proxy statement that Theriva now admits "contained errors." Those are his allegations. The Court has not ruled on them.

Theriva is represented by two law firms and opposes his request to stop a special stockholder meeting set for November 18, 2026. The Court denied an earlier request from him. His current request is fully briefed and waiting for a decision. Theriva's answer to the complaint is due December 1.

He holds 90,191 Theriva shares. His complaint says that by his brokerage records they are worth $759,000 less than he paid for them. He has lived in his truck since August 2024, as he told the Court in a filing in the case.

He wrote in the complaint about the people he is bringing the case for, the stockholders of small companies: "These are people who believe the press releases they read."

He did the work with an AI assistant, Claude. It helped him read the court's rules, check his citations against the sources and assemble the filings. He reads and signs every one himself. "I am known for a good fight," he said. "I am not an attorney." He plans to meet attorneys in Las Vegas in the coming weeks and says he would welcome a young lawyer willing to take a shot. "It's all about the people," he said.

Every paper he files in the case is posted, as filed, at nejmehvstheriva.com.

If you are thinking of it

Many law schools run clinics that help people in court for free. Bar associations run lawyer referral services, and some federal courts have help desks for people who represent themselves. Every federal court publishes its own local rules, and those rules decide page limits, formats and deadlines. Read them before you file anything.

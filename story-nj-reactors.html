How New Jersey opened the door to four small reactors without an approved design

The state changed its nuclear law this year. The reactor planned for Oyster Creek has not cleared federal review.

By Mark Nejmeh · Newsweed · November 5, 2026

Holtec plans to build four small reactors at the old Oyster Creek plant in Lacey Township, 1,360 megawatts in all, and is aiming for 2036. The design is its own, the SMR-300. Holtec itself says it has not built one and does not yet have all the regulatory approvals it needs.

The state moved first

In April, Gov. Mikie Sherrill signed a law removing the restriction that had blocked new nuclear plants in New Jersey for decades. The state can now approve permits based on safe on-site storage of radioactive waste, rather than waiting for a federal permanent repository.

On July 13 she signed the Power NJ Act, which sets up a state process for choosing nuclear projects. It opens by January 9, 2027, with a final board order due by July 2028. The act supports at least 1,100 megawatts, but it says that figure "shall not constitute a commitment by the state to procure any specific quantity of generation." No reactor has been ordered.

What the federal government has and has not approved

On July 30, Holtec cleared a federal step at Oyster Creek: the Nuclear Regulatory Commission approved its plan for finishing the old plant's cleanup. That approval is not for the new reactors. Holtec has not disclosed a construction permit schedule for the site.

The SMR-300 is under review at Palisades in Michigan, Holtec's first planned site. On August 28 the NRC let Holtec install excavation support and cutoff walls there. That was not a construction permit. Holtec's route is allowed, because the NRC reviews a design as part of the permit application. But New Jersey's laws are on the books, and the federal answer on this reactor is not in.

Other companies are further along on paper. In 2023 the NRC certified a small reactor design from NuScale, and in May 2025 it approved an updated version with larger modules. Approval is not a building. The NRC says a company using that design would still have to apply for permission to build and operate a reactor.

Salem, and how the count could reach six

PSEG holds federal pre-approval for one or two more reactors in Salem County. Added to Holtec's four, the count could reach six, though nothing says Salem's would be small. Sherrill has described Salem as close to the most ready site. She likes the idea of small reactors at Oyster Creek, but she is skeptical of the technology Holtec is pushing and wants large reactors built first as proof. PSEG's chief executive has said a project like this will take many partners.

The money

The law's sponsor, Sen. John Burzichelli, put a new plant at about $24 billion. The state's Division of Rate Counsel projects about $7 more on the average monthly electric bill if a plant comes in on time and on budget, and about $22 with overruns like those in Georgia. Those costs could last up to 40 years.

The law requires federal financing first, and the governor's office says customers pay nothing until a plant is delivering power and will not be responsible for overruns. The Rate Counsel's director has said he worries ratepayers become the backstop if the federal money does not arrive. It is not clear the Trump administration will provide it.

Holtec is also raising money from investors. In September, Holtec Nuclear set terms for an $825 million stock offering on Nasdaq. And Holtec has a seat at the table: Patrick O'Brien, its director of government affairs and communications, sits on the governor's nuclear task force, which first met October 1. He told that meeting the plants are years away and will need financial support.

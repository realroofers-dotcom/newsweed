GoodSolar: what it is, and how to survive as a nomad on the power of the sun

Van, truck, RV, boat or tent: the sun is the one power source that follows you everywhere. Here is how to use it well.

20 feet

GoodSolar is power you use right where the sun lands: the panel, a short cable, a battery and the thing it runs, all within about 20 feet. Short runs lose less power, cost less and keep the system simple enough to set up yourself. On the road, that is exactly the system you carry.

1. Know what you need to run

Everything starts with your daily use. Write down what you run and for how long, then multiply.

The math: watts × hours used per day = watt-hours (Wh) per day. Add them up. Example: a 60 W laptop for 4 hours is 240 Wh; ten 5 W LED lights for 5 hours is 250 Wh; phones a little more. Your fridge is usually the biggest steady load, so read its label.

The usual nomad list: a fridge, lights, a fan, phones and a laptop, an internet router, a water pump, and for some, a CPAP machine. Heating and cooking with electricity take far more power; most travelers use gas or a stove for those.

2. Pick the battery

The easiest way is a portable power station, a battery with the outlets and the solar charger built in. Small ones (a few hundred Wh) cover phones, lights and a laptop. For a fridge and a full day of living, travelers usually carry 1,000 to 2,000 Wh or more. Larger systems add extra batteries. Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries last many more charging cycles.

3. Size the panels

A rough rule: on a clear day a panel makes about 4 to 5 times its rated watts in watt-hours, less in winter, shade or clouds. A 200 W panel can bring in roughly 600 to 1,000 Wh on a sunny day. Two ways to carry them:

  • On the roof: rigid panels charge whenever the sun is on the vehicle, driving or parked.
  • Folding panels: park in the shade to stay cool and set the panel out in the sun on a short cable. Tilt it toward the sun.

Have a second way to charge for cloudy weeks: from the vehicle while driving (a car charging cable or a DC-to-DC charger) or from a campground outlet when you have one.

4. Park for the sun

  • Look for open sky to the south (in the northern hemisphere) and no trees or buildings throwing shade at midday.
  • Even a little shade on part of a panel cuts its power a lot.
  • Wipe the dust off. A dirty panel makes less.
  • In winter the sun is low: tilt folding panels up more steeply.

5. Mind the cold and the heat

Most lithium batteries should not be charged below freezing (32°F, 0°C); many power stations stop charging on their own until they warm up. Keep the battery inside the living space in winter. In summer, keep it out of direct sun and out of a closed, baking vehicle when you can; heat shortens battery life.

6. Stretch every watt

  • Use LED lights and 12-volt devices straight from the battery’s car-style or USB outlets; turning DC into household AC wastes some power.
  • Switch the AC outlets off when nothing is plugged in.
  • Run the big things (charging the laptop, the coffee maker) in the middle of the day while the sun is refilling the battery.

7. Stay safe

  • Fuse the positive wire close to the battery, and use cable thick enough for the current.
  • Tie panels down for driving. A panel that comes off at highway speed is dangerous.
  • Never run a gas generator inside or next to an open window; carbon monoxide kills. That is one more reason to go solar.
  • Anything wired into an RV’s own electrical panel is a job for someone who knows the wiring.
